# Literature Survey Plan

## Study Context

This literature survey supports the study **"Diagnostic Errors in Pathology and Their Medico-Legal Consequences: A Mixed Legal and Clinical Review of Indian Case Law."** It maps the existing scholarship across six thematic domains, identifies key sources and research gaps, and provides a phased execution plan for conducting the survey.

---

## 1. Thematic Structure

The literature survey is organized into six thematic domains. Each domain contributes to a distinct aspect of the study's background, methodology, or analysis.

### Domain B: Medical Negligence Law in India — Landmark Cases and Legal Principles

**Purpose:** Document the Indian legal framework governing medical negligence, including judicial tests, burden of proof, and the distinction between civil and criminal liability.

**Landmark Supreme Court Cases:**

| # | Case Name | Year | Key Principle |
|---|-----------|------|--------------|
| B1 | Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha, AIR 1995 SC 550 | 1995 | Medical services under the Consumer Protection Act 1986; patients paying fees are "consumers" |
| B2 | Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab, (2005) 6 SCC 1 | 2005 | Adopted Bolam test for India; criminal negligence requires "gross negligence"; credible expert opinion needed before criminal charges |
| B3 | Kusum Sharma v. Batra Hospital, (2010) INSC 95 | 2010 | Negligence not inferred from poor outcome alone; error of judgment is not negligence if course was reasonable |
| B4 | Malay Kumar Ganguly v. Dr. Sukumar Mukherjee, AIR 2010 SC 1162 | 2009 | No guarantee of cure; adoption of one accepted treatment mode is not negligence |
| B5 | P.B. Desai v. State of Maharashtra | 2013 | Distinguished professional misconduct from criminal negligence; reversed conviction |
| B6 | Neeraj Sud v. Jaswinder Singh, (2024) INSC 825 | 2024 | Most recent reaffirmation of Bolam test; post-surgical deterioration not negligence without evidence of departure from accepted standards |

**Key Legal Doctrines to Cover:**
- Bolam test (practice accepted by responsible body of professionals)
- Bolitho refinement (accepted practice must have logical basis)

- Consumer Protection Act coverage of medical services (IMA v. Shantha framework)
- Expert evidence requirements (Jacob Mathew guidelines)

## 2. Identified Research Gaps

The literature survey reveals the following gaps that this study is positioned to address:

1. **No systematic review of Indian pathology-specific litigation exists.** Most Indian studies examine medical negligence broadly across all specialties, with pathology cases appearing only as incidental examples.

2. **Pathology error frameworks are predominantly Western.** The Raab, Nakhleh, and Plebani taxonomies were developed from US and European data. Their applicability to Indian pathology practice settings (varying accreditation levels, resource constraints, workforce ratios) has not been systematically evaluated.

3. **Consumer court pathology decisions are undocumented as a corpus.** Individual cases (e.g., Lifeline Laboratory, Dr Lal Pathlabs) have been reported in news media, but no academic study has systematically collected, coded, and analyzed these decisions.

4. **The interaction between NABL accreditation status and litigation outcomes is unexplored.** Whether laboratory accreditation influences judicial assessment of standard of care in Indian pathology disputes has not been studied.

5. **Compensation patterns for diagnostic error cases in India are unknown.** While specialty-level compensation data exists (e.g., highest average payout in anaesthesiology at Rs 13.78 lakhs), no data isolates pathology or diagnostic laboratory compensation trends.

6. **The Bolam test's application to diagnostic interpretation errors is understudied.** While the test is well-analyzed for treatment decisions, its specific application to pathology interpretation disagreements (where multiple reasonable interpretations may exist) has not been systematically examined.

## 3. Execution Plan

### Phase 1: Systematic Database Searches (Weeks 1-3)

Execute the search strategy defined in `config/literature_search_strategy.yml` across all configured sources.

**Priority order:**
1. PubMed/MEDLINE — biomedical and diagnostic error literature
2. Google Scholar — broader coverage including Indian journals, law reviews, and case commentaries
3. Indian legal databases (Indian Kanoon, SCC Online, Manupatra) — case commentaries and law journal articles
4. IndMED — Indian biomedical index
5. Institutional library discovery tools

**Search execution protocol:**
- Run each query template from `config/literature_search_strategy.yml`
- Log every search in `data/metadata/literature_search_log_template.csv`
- Export raw results to `data/raw/literature/`
- Enter each record into `data/metadata/literature_inventory_template.csv`

### Phase 2: Screening and Eligibility (Weeks 3-5)

1. Title/abstract screening against inclusion criteria in `docs/literature_review_tracking_plan.md`
2. Full-text retrieval for potentially eligible records
3. Full-text eligibility screening
4. Deduplication across databases
5. Document exclusion reasons for all excluded records

### Phase 3: Data Extraction (Weeks 5-7)

1. Extract fields defined in `data/metadata/literature_extraction_template.csv` for each included record
2. Identify citation leads (Indian cases cited in literature) and cross-reference against the case inventory
3. Tag each record with relevance categories: background, methods, discussion, recommendations

### Phase 4: Thematic Synthesis (Weeks 7-9)

Synthesize extracted literature into the six thematic domains:

1. **Global diagnostic error prevalence and malpractice patterns** — summarize error rates, claim patterns, and risk factors from Domain A sources
2. **Indian medical negligence legal framework** — synthesize judicial principles, landmark holdings, and doctrinal evolution from Domain B sources
3. **Pathology litigation in India** — compile and analyze available case reports and commentaries from Domain C sources
4. **Diagnostic error classification and quality standards** — present applicable frameworks and their relevance to the Indian context from Domain D sources
5. **Risk management strategies** — derive evidence-based recommendations from Domain E sources
6. **Consumer court trends and expert evidence** — characterize the adjudicatory landscape from Domain F sources

### Phase 5: Gap Analysis and Manuscript Integration (Weeks 9-10)

1. Finalize the research gap statement based on what the literature does and does not cover
2. Position the present study within the identified gaps
3. Produce a tracked bibliography of included and excluded records
4. Generate a citation-lead table mapping literature-cited Indian cases against the case inventory
5. Draft the literature review section of the manuscript
