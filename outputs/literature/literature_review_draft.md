# Literature Review

## Diagnostic Errors in Pathology and Their Medico-Legal Consequences: A Mixed Legal and Clinical Review of Indian Case Law

---

### 1. Introduction

Pathology underpins modern clinical decision-making. An estimated seventy per cent of medical decisions depend on laboratory data, and histopathological, cytological, and autopsy findings routinely determine whether patients undergo surgery, chemotherapy, surveillance, or palliation (Lippi and Plebani, 2022). Because the consequences of a wrong pathology report can be irreversible, diagnostic errors in pathology occupy a distinctive position in both patient-safety science and medico-legal analysis. This review synthesizes the existing scholarship across six interrelated domains: the epidemiology of diagnostic errors in pathology worldwide; the legal framework governing medical negligence in India; published evidence on pathology-specific litigation in India; clinical taxonomies for classifying diagnostic errors; quality-assurance standards applicable to pathology practice; and medico-legal risk-management strategies. Its purpose is to locate the present study within the existing literature, identify gaps that justify a systematic examination of Indian case law, and provide the conceptual foundation for integrating legal and clinical analysis.

---

### 2. Diagnostic Errors in Pathology: Global Prevalence and Patterns

Diagnostic error in pathology is neither rare nor trivial. The magnitude, however, depends heavily on the method of measurement, the subspecialty examined, and the clinical context in which discrepancies are detected.

#### 2.1 Error Rates in Surgical Pathology and Cytology

Large-scale quality-assurance studies conducted through the College of American Pathologists (CAP) Q-Probes programme have established baseline benchmarks for diagnostic discrepancy in anatomic pathology. Raab and colleagues (2005) reported a mean discrepancy frequency of 6.7 per cent in a multi-institutional secondary-review study, with breast and bronchial cytologic specimens showing the highest discrepancy percentages. In a parallel study, the same group estimated a twelve per cent error rate in cancer diagnosis specifically, of which 5.3 per cent had moderate or marked clinical impact on patient management (Raab et al., 2005). Renshaw (2007) compared different measurement methods in real-world surgical pathology practice and found that major error rates ranged from 1.5 to 5.7 per cent for institutional consultations and from 0.26 to 1.2 per cent for in-house prospective review, demonstrating that the apparent frequency of error is strongly method-dependent.

Amended-report rates provide another window into diagnostic error. Nakhleh and Zarbo (1998), in a CAP Q-Probes study of 1,667,547 surgical pathology specimens across 359 laboratories, found a mean amended-report rate of 1.9 per 1,000 cases. Of these, 38.7 per cent were issued to change the final diagnosis and 19.2 per cent corrected patient-identification errors. Clinician requests accounted for 20.5 per cent of error detections, suggesting that a substantial proportion of diagnostic errors are first identified outside the laboratory.

The most recent meta-analytic data come from a 2025 systematic review of technical errors in pathology tissue testing, which reported pooled error rates of 16.71 per 1,000 cases for pre-analytic errors and 40.17 per 1,000 for actively identified laboratory errors. Reported errors were approximately eight-fold lower than actively identified errors, indicating significant underreporting in routine practice.

#### 2.2 Cytology and FNAC

Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) occupies a particularly sensitive position in medico-legal terms because it frequently serves as the initial diagnostic modality for palpable masses, especially in the breast and thyroid. Cazzaniga and colleagues (2019), in a systematic review of thyroid FNAC misdiagnosis, identified sampling errors from inadequate or improperly collected specimens and interpretive variability between pathologists as the two principal drivers of false-positive and false-negative results. These findings are consistent with the malpractice-claims literature, where breast FNA false negatives have been identified as one of the most common bases for litigation (Troxel, 2000).

#### 2.3 Autopsy-Detected Diagnostic Errors

Autopsy studies provide the most stringent test of diagnostic accuracy, since they compare ante-mortem clinical diagnoses against post-mortem findings. In a landmark systematic review of 53 autopsy series spanning 1966 to 2002, Shojania and colleagues (2003) reported a median major diagnostic error rate of 23.5 per cent and a median Class I error rate (errors that, had they been detected during life, would have changed management and potentially altered outcome) of 9.0 per cent. Although relative decreases of approximately nineteen per cent for major errors and thirty-three per cent for Class I errors per decade were observed, the absolute rates remained substantial. A more recent meta-analysis by Carlotti and colleagues (2024), focused on ICU autopsies across 42 studies, found a pooled Class I discrepancy rate of 6.5 per cent and a major missed-diagnosis rate of 19.3 per cent, with no significant decline over time. These data confirm that autopsy-related disputes, including those involving forensic pathology, arise against a background of persistent diagnostic uncertainty.

#### 2.4 Second Opinions and Diagnostic Discrepancy

Second-opinion review has emerged as a principal quality-assurance mechanism for detecting diagnostic error. Kuijpers and colleagues (2018), in a comprehensive review, found that second-opinion reviews detect major discrepancies in 6.2 per cent of cases, with cases discussed at multidisciplinary team meetings showing an eight-fold higher detection rate compared with cases not subjected to review. Kronz, Westra, and Epstein (1999) studied mandatory second-opinion surgical pathology at a large referral hospital and found 1.4 per cent major diagnostic disagreements that prompted changes in clinical management. Middleton and colleagues (2018) reported an overall discordance rate of 14.2 per cent (2.2 per cent major, 12.0 per cent minor) in a study of 8,191 outside surgical pathology cases reviewed at a comprehensive cancer centre. Chaudry and colleagues (2021) confirmed that institutionalized second-opinion practices detect clinically significant discrepancies and support improved patient outcomes, particularly in oncologic pathology. Taken together, these findings establish that a non-trivial proportion of pathology diagnoses are revised upon review, and that the absence of review mechanisms increases the risk of undetected error.

### 3. Malpractice Litigation Involving Pathologists: International Evidence

The relationship between diagnostic error and malpractice liability in pathology has been documented most extensively in the United States, where insurer data and legal databases provide structured records of claims and outcomes.

#### 3.1 Claims Patterns

Troxel, drawing on data from The Doctors Company, published a series of analyses spanning two decades of pathology malpractice claims. In an initial review of 378 claims from 1998 to 2003, fifty-seven per cent involved breast specimens, melanoma, Pap smears, gynaecologic specimens, or operational errors, and sixty-three per cent concerned a failure to diagnose cancer (Troxel, 2005). Claim frequency for pathologists was low relative to other specialties, but claim severity (the magnitude of individual payouts) was high. A subsequent analysis of 276 claims from 2004 to 2010 documented a decline in melanoma, breast, and gynaecologic cytology claims, which Troxel (2012) attributed to improved continuing medical education. By 2019, Troxel reported that diagnostic errors accounted for eighty-seven per cent of all paid pathology malpractice claims, with melanoma misdiagnosis remaining the single leading allegation, followed by breast-cancer false negatives, prostate-cancer grading errors, and cervical Pap-smear false negatives.

Kornstein and Byrne (2007), in an independent analysis of 171 legal cases from the LexisNexis database spanning 1988 to 2005, confirmed that nearly half of pathology-related lawsuits involved surgical pathology, with cytology and clinical pathology accounting for the remainder. Missed melanoma on skin biopsy was again the most common single allegation.

#### 3.2 Implications for the Indian Context

Although the Indian legal system differs substantially from the American tort framework, the international claims data carry several implications for Indian scholarship. First, the concentration of claims around a limited number of specimen types and diagnoses (melanoma, breast, cervical, prostate, thyroid) suggests that similar concentrations may exist in Indian litigation, albeit potentially weighted toward different disease profiles given differences in cancer epidemiology. Second, the finding that diagnostic errors rather than procedural or operational failures dominate paid claims underscores the importance of studying interpretive error specifically. Third, the demonstrated effect of continuing education on reducing claims suggests that educational interventions may be relevant in the Indian context as well.

### 4. Medical Negligence Law in India: Legal Framework and Judicial Principles

The legal framework governing medical negligence in India has evolved through a series of landmark Supreme Court decisions, legislative enactments, and doctrinal analyses. Understanding this framework is essential for interpreting how Indian courts evaluate pathology-related diagnostic disputes.

#### 4.1 Consumer Protection Act and the Inclusion of Medical Services

The foundational event in Indian medical negligence litigation was the Supreme Court's decision in *Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha* (1995), which held that medical services rendered for a fee constitute "services" within the meaning of the Consumer Protection Act, 1986, and that patients who pay for such services are "consumers" entitled to seek redress through consumer dispute redressal forums. This decision effectively opened a parallel adjudicatory pathway for medical negligence claims outside the civil and criminal court systems, significantly lowering the procedural barriers to litigation. The Consumer Protection Act, 2019, which replaced the 1986 Act, continued this coverage. Section 2(11) of the 2019 Act expressly includes negligence or omission causing loss or injury within the definition of "deficiency in service," while Section 69(1) imposes a two-year limitation period for filing complaints.

#### 4.2 The Bolam Test and Its Indian Adoption

The standard for assessing whether a medical professional has been negligent was settled in *Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab* (2005), in which the Supreme Court formally adopted the Bolam test for Indian medical negligence jurisprudence. Under this standard, a doctor is not negligent if acting in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical professionals skilled in that particular art, even if other practitioners would have adopted a different approach. The Court further held that for criminal prosecution of a medical professional, the conduct must rise to the level of "gross negligence" or "recklessness," far exceeding the threshold for civil liability. To prevent frivolous criminal complaints, the Court required a credible expert opinion before criminal charges could proceed.

The Bolam test was comprehensively restated in *Kusum Sharma v. Batra Hospital* (2010), where the Supreme Court consolidated several key principles: negligence cannot be inferred from a poor outcome alone; an error of judgment does not constitute negligence if the chosen course of action was reasonable; and the standard is that of an ordinary competent practitioner exercising ordinary skill, not the highest skill available. In *Malay Kumar Ganguly v. Dr. Sukumar Mukherjee* (2009), the Court reinforced that no doctor guarantees a cure and that adopting one among several accepted modes of treatment does not constitute negligence. Most recently, in *Neeraj Sud v. Jaswinder Singh* (2024), the Supreme Court reaffirmed the Bolam test and held that deterioration of a patient's condition following surgery is not indicative of negligence in the absence of additional evidence demonstrating departure from accepted standards.

The Bolitho refinement, which requires that the accepted practice relied upon must have a logical basis, has been discussed in Indian legal scholarship but has received less direct judicial application. Scholarly analyses have noted the tension between the Bolam test's deference to professional opinion and the Bolitho requirement of logical defensibility, suggesting that Indian courts may increasingly scrutinize whether expert opinions offered in defence are logically sustainable.

#### 4.3 Criminal Versus Civil Negligence

The distinction between criminal and civil liability was further clarified in *P.B. Desai v. State of Maharashtra* (2013), where the Supreme Court reversed a criminal conviction under Section 338 of the Indian Penal Code, holding that the prosecution had failed to prove criminal negligence beyond a reasonable doubt. This decision reinforced the high threshold for criminal liability established in *Jacob Mathew* and confirmed that professional misconduct falling short of gross negligence should be addressed through civil or disciplinary channels rather than criminal prosecution.

#### 4.4 Expert Evidence

Indian courts have recognized the importance of expert medical evidence in negligence determinations. The National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (NCDRC) has held that expert evidence is crucial in establishing or refuting allegations of negligence, with exceptions under the *res ipsa loquitur* doctrine where negligence is self-evident (such as wrong-site surgery or retained foreign bodies). A recent cross-sectional study by Sethi and Shringarpure (2025), analysing twenty-six Indian court judgments, found that all types of Indian courts, including consumer forums, accept published medical journal articles as a form of expert evidence, and that the impact factor of the cited journal did not influence case outcomes. A ten-year review of Supreme Court medical negligence decisions found that medical records were a decisive factor in 87.5 per cent of all verdicts favouring doctors or hospitals, underscoring the importance of documentation quality in the Indian legal context.

#### 4.5 Res Ipsa Loquitur

The doctrine of *res ipsa loquitur* — which allows negligence to be inferred from the circumstances without specific proof of the defendant's conduct — has been held by Indian courts to be applicable only in limited situations where the negligence is self-evident, such as amputation of the wrong limb or retention of a surgical instrument within the patient's body. The Supreme Court has clarified that the doctrine does not apply merely because treatment fails or an adverse outcome occurs. When the doctrine does apply, the burden shifts to the healthcare provider to explain the occurrence. The application of this doctrine to diagnostic errors in pathology, where the distinction between negligence and acceptable interpretive disagreement is inherently complex, has not been systematically examined.

### 5. Pathology-Specific Litigation in India: Existing Evidence

Despite the well-documented rise in medical negligence litigation in India and the centrality of pathology to clinical decision-making, systematic academic study of pathology-specific litigation remains conspicuously absent. The available evidence is fragmented across isolated case reports, broader reviews of medical negligence that include pathology cases incidentally, and media reports of individual consumer court decisions.

#### 5.1 Published Academic Studies

The most systematic study of consumer court negligence decisions relevant to this field is a five-year retrospective review of 253 NCDRC cases from 2015 to 2019, published in the Indian Journal of Medical Ethics (2023). This study found that negligence was proved in 53 per cent of cases, with the most common error being lack of skill or care (36 per cent), followed by deficient medical records (22 per cent). Surgery dominated both the caseload (29 per cent) and the convictions (27 per cent). While this study did not isolate pathology or diagnostic laboratory cases as a separate category, its finding that wrong diagnosis constitutes a recognized form of deficiency in service establishes the legal basis for pathology-related claims.

A separate records review of 942 NCDRC cases from 2002 to 2018, published in the Egyptian Journal of Neurosurgery (2025), demonstrated that specialty-specific analysis of consumer court decisions is methodologically feasible and can yield meaningful data on litigation patterns, conviction rates, and compensation trends. This study, focused on neurosurgery and neurology, provides a direct methodological precedent for the pathology-specific analysis proposed in the present study.

The single most directly relevant published case study is a 2002 medico-legal report describing a fifty-year-old woman whose breast lump was diagnosed as malignant by FNAC but found to be a benign phyllodes tumour on subsequent histopathological examination, a finding confirmed by centres in India and the United Kingdom (PubMed, PMID 12437194). The patient filed a complaint with the State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission seeking approximately Rs 19 lakh in damages. This case encapsulates the central tension in pathology-related litigation: the FNAC finding represented a false-positive diagnosis with significant clinical consequences, yet the question of whether the error constituted negligence or fell within the inherent limitations of cytological interpretation requires both legal and clinical analysis.

#### 5.2 Reported Consumer Court Decisions

Several individual consumer court decisions involving diagnostic laboratories have been reported in legal and medical news media. The NCDRC reduced compensation from Rs 20 lakh to Rs 4 lakh against Lifeline Laboratory for issuing an incorrect blood group report that led to a kidney transplant being deferred. In a separate case, Dr Lal Pathlabs was ordered to pay Rs 3.5 lakh for an erroneous laboratory report that caused unnecessary emergency hospitalization. A Mumbai woman who was wrongly diagnosed with breast cancer based on a histopathology report and underwent mastectomy filed a consumer court complaint seeking Rs 50 lakh in compensation. A Nagpur laboratory was ordered to compensate patients for issuing wrong diagnostic reports. These cases, while individually documented, have not been collected, coded, or analysed as a corpus, and their implications for understanding judicial reasoning about pathology-specific negligence remain unexamined.

#### 5.3 Histopathology Review Data from India

A 2022 study from a tertiary oncology hospital in Eastern India documented diagnostic discrepancies upon histopathology review of referred specimens, including cases of incorrect grading. While this study was not framed as a medico-legal analysis, its findings are relevant because they demonstrate that pathology diagnostic discrepancies occur in Indian practice at rates that are broadly consistent with international benchmarks. Such discrepancies, when they result in changes to clinical management, form the factual substrate for potential medico-legal disputes.

#### 5.4 Systemic Quality Concerns

A 2020 investigation reported in The Week (India) documented systemic problems in Indian pathology laboratories, including arrangements between doctors and laboratories involving referral commissions, "sink tests" where specimens are discarded and fabricated results issued, and other forms of fraudulent reporting. These practices, where they lead to patient harm, represent a category of alleged negligence that is qualitatively different from interpretive error and may be evaluated under different legal standards. Their prevalence and their relationship to litigation patterns have not been studied.

### 6. Diagnostic Error Frameworks and Quality Assurance Standards

The classification and prevention of diagnostic errors in pathology are supported by several internationally recognized frameworks and quality-assurance programmes.

#### 6.1 The Total Testing Process and Phase-Based Error Taxonomy

Plebani's work on laboratory error has established the dominant taxonomic framework, dividing the diagnostic process into pre-analytical, analytical, and post-analytical phases. In a foundational 2010 study, Plebani reported that pre-analytical errors account for 46 to 68 per cent of total laboratory errors, analytical errors for 7 to 13 per cent, and post-analytical errors for the remainder. Lippi and Guidi (2007) extended this taxonomy to include "pre-pre-analytical" errors (arising during test ordering and patient preparation before specimen collection) and "post-post-analytical" errors (arising during result interpretation and clinical follow-up after result delivery), demonstrating that the phases with the highest error incidence lie outside the laboratory's direct control.

For anatomic pathology specifically, Raab and colleagues (2006) proposed an evidence-based error classification with four categories: misinterpretations, misidentifications, specimen defects, and report defects. This taxonomy achieved good interobserver agreement and was designed to support standardized measurement of error frequencies and clinical impact. In a subsequent study, Raab and colleagues (2010) applied this framework and found overall diagnostic error rates of 1.7 to 3.7 per cent, with major errors at 0.3 to 0.5 per cent and minor errors at 1.2 to 3.3 per cent.

#### 6.2 The IOM Diagnostic Error Framework

The 2015 report *Improving Diagnosis in Health Care* by the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine provided the most widely cited contemporary definition of diagnostic error as "the failure to establish an accurate and timely explanation of the patient's health problem(s) or communicate that explanation to the patient." The report's framework explicitly linked diagnostic process errors — including pathology specimen mix-ups — to missed, delayed, or wrong diagnoses and to adverse patient outcomes. This framework is relevant to the present study because it encompasses not only interpretive errors but also communication failures, reporting delays, and system-level failures, all of which have been alleged in Indian pathology litigation.

#### 6.3 CAP Quality Programmes

The College of American Pathologists has operated two complementary quality programmes: Q-Probes, a series of cross-sectional benchmarking studies, and Q-Tracks, a longitudinal monitoring programme. Over fifteen years, these programmes established provisional benchmarks for quality practice and demonstrated that continuous assessment measurably lowers error rates, particularly for intraoperative consultation (Howanitz, 2005). In 2016, the CAP and the Association of Directors of Anatomic and Surgical Pathology (ADASP) published an evidence-based guideline recommending that anatomic pathologists develop case-review procedures to detect disagreements and potential interpretive errors (Meier et al., 2016). A 2014 Q-Probes study of 360,218 surgical pathology cases found an aggregate report-defect rate of 4.7 per 1,000 cases and demonstrated that pre-sign-out diagnostic slide review was associated with lower amended-report rates.

#### 6.4 NABL Accreditation and Indian Quality Standards

In India, the National Accreditation Board for Testing and Calibration Laboratories (NABL), operating under the Quality Council of India, provides accreditation for medical laboratories against ISO 15189, the international standard for quality and competence in medical laboratories. Accreditation covers pre-analytical, analytical, and post-analytical processes and is granted for a two-year cycle. Laboratories are currently transitioning to ISO 15189:2022, with a deadline of December 2025. NABL accreditation is voluntary for most laboratories in India, which means that a substantial number of diagnostic laboratories operate without formal quality accreditation. Whether the accreditation status of a laboratory has influenced judicial assessment of standard of care in Indian pathology disputes has not been studied — a gap that the present study is positioned to explore.

### 7. Medico-Legal Risk Management in Pathology

The literature on reducing medico-legal risk in pathology converges on several evidence-based strategies, though the evidence base is predominantly derived from Western practice settings.

#### 7.1 Documentation as Defence

Documentation quality emerges as the single most important factor in the legal defence of pathology practice. A ten-year review of Supreme Court of India medical negligence decisions found that medical records were decisive in 87.5 per cent of all verdicts favouring the defendant (2024). Nakhleh (2006) argued that the quality of the pathology report is the primary defence in malpractice litigation and advocated for synoptic reporting formats that standardize the information conveyed to clinicians. Deficient medical records were the second most common error category (22 per cent) in a five-year review of NCDRC cases (2023), indicating that documentation failures are not merely a theoretical risk but a demonstrated pathway to adverse judicial findings.

#### 7.2 Communication Protocols

Communication failures between pathologists and clinicians have been identified as a distinct category of medico-legal risk. Nakhleh (2006, 2007) emphasized the importance of documenting communication of critical and unexpected diagnoses to the ordering clinician, particularly for findings that require urgent clinical action. The IOM framework (2015) similarly identified failure to communicate diagnostic findings as a recognized diagnostic process error with potential for patient harm.

#### 7.3 Second-Opinion and Review Mechanisms

As discussed in Section 2.4, the evidence consistently supports second-opinion review as a mechanism for detecting clinically significant diagnostic discrepancies. From a risk-management perspective, the absence of a review mechanism may be viewed as a system-level failure that increases exposure to both diagnostic error and litigation. The CAP/ADASP guideline (2016) recommended institutionalized case-review procedures, and the empirical evidence from Kuijpers (2018), Middleton (2018), and others supports the value of mandatory review, particularly for cancer diagnoses.

#### 7.4 Risk Management in Indian Pathology Laboratories

The literature on risk management specific to Indian pathology practice is sparse. Mehta and colleagues (2024), in the only identified Indian study directly addressing pathology laboratory risk management, classified risks in a tertiary hospital clinical pathology laboratory into natural calamity, environmental, manpower-related, pre-analytical, analytical, post-analytical, and laboratory hazard categories. The study found that inadequate sample-to-anticoagulant ratio had the highest probability of occurrence (22.85 per cent), followed by quantity insufficient for analysis (7.30 per cent) and laboratory information system breakdown (6.58 per cent). While this study did not address medico-legal risk directly, it demonstrates the types of pre-analytical and analytical vulnerabilities that may form the basis for negligence allegations in Indian consumer courts.

### 8. Indian Consumer Courts and Medical Negligence: Trends and Patterns

The consumer dispute redressal system — comprising District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions, State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions, and the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (NCDRC) — has become the primary forum for medical negligence claims in India, offering procedural simplicity, lower costs, and faster resolution compared with civil courts.

#### 8.1 Caseload and Outcomes

The most comprehensive analysis of NCDRC medical negligence decisions is the five-year retrospective review of 253 cases from 2015 to 2019 (IJME, 2023). Negligence was proved in 135 cases (53 per cent). Surgery accounted for the largest share of both cases (73, or 29 per cent) and convictions (37, or 27 per cent). The most common type of error was lack of skill or care (62 cases, 36 per cent), followed by deficient medical records (38 cases, 22 per cent). At the state commission level, a study of 89 cases in Chandigarh, Punjab, and Haryana (Modgil, Dasari, and Singh, 2021) and a study of district consumer court cases in Delhi (Yadav and Rastogi, 2015) provided additional data on litigation patterns at lower tiers of the consumer forum system. A commentary published in the Indian Journal of Ophthalmology (2023) noted an alarming upward trend in consumer court medical malpractice claims, attributing the increase to growing patient awareness of legal rights under consumer protection law.

#### 8.2 Compensation

Compensation awarded in Indian medical negligence cases varies widely. The NCDRC five-year review reported a highest single payout of Rs 1.38 crore (in a paediatric case) and Rs 1.1 crore (in an obstetrics/gynaecology case), with the highest average payout by specialty in anaesthesiology at Rs 13.78 lakhs. Compensation patterns specific to pathology or diagnostic laboratory cases have not been isolated in the published literature. Individual reported decisions suggest a range from Rs 3.5 lakh (Dr Lal Pathlabs erroneous report) to Rs 4 lakh (Lifeline Laboratory wrong blood group) to claims of Rs 50 lakh (Mumbai breast cancer misdiagnosis), but these represent isolated data points rather than systematic compensation analysis.

#### 8.3 Mortality Data

An analysis of National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) data from 2017 to 2023 identified 1,133 deaths attributed to alleged medical negligence over the seven-year period, with the highest number (218, or 19.24 per cent) in 2018 (ResearchGate, 2024). These data reflect cases registered under criminal law and represent a distinct subset of medical negligence allegations from those adjudicated in consumer forums. The proportion attributable to diagnostic errors in pathology is not disaggregated.

### 9. Research Gaps and Justification for the Present Study

The literature reviewed above reveals several critical gaps that the present study is designed to address.

**First, no systematic review of Indian pathology-specific litigation exists.** The available evidence consists of isolated case reports, general medical negligence reviews that include pathology cases incidentally, and media accounts of individual consumer court decisions. No study has systematically collected, coded, and analysed Indian judicial decisions involving pathologists, pathology laboratories, or diagnostic laboratories as a distinct corpus.

**Second, the interaction between diagnostic error taxonomies and Indian legal standards has not been examined.** The Raab, Plebani, and IOM frameworks for classifying diagnostic errors were developed from Western data and have not been applied to the analysis of Indian pathology litigation. Whether Indian courts distinguish between interpretive errors, pre-analytical failures, communication breakdowns, and system-level deficiencies — or treat them as a single undifferentiated category of negligence — remains unknown.

**Third, the application of the Bolam test to pathology interpretive disputes has not been systematically studied.** While the Bolam test is well-analysed for treatment decisions, its application to diagnostic interpretation disagreements — where multiple reasonable interpretations of a histopathological or cytological specimen may exist — raises distinct questions. How Indian courts determine whether a diagnostic interpretation was "accepted by a responsible body of medical professionals" in the context of known inter-observer variability in pathology has not been explored.

**Fourth, the relationship between NABL accreditation and judicial assessment of standard of care is unexplored.** Given that NABL accreditation is voluntary for most Indian laboratories, a substantial proportion of pathology negligence claims may involve unaccredited facilities. Whether accreditation status influences judicial reasoning about the expected standard of care has not been documented.

**Fifth, compensation patterns for diagnostic error cases in India are unknown at the pathology-specific level.** While specialty-level data exist, no study isolates compensation trends for pathology or diagnostic laboratory disputes.

**Sixth, the clinical basis of alleged errors in Indian pathology litigation has not been independently assessed.** Published studies report legal outcomes but do not systematically evaluate, from a clinical pathology perspective, whether the alleged errors represented negligence, diagnostic limitations inherent to the technique, system-level failures, or acceptable differences in professional interpretation.

The present study addresses these gaps through a mixed legal and clinical review of reported Indian judicial decisions involving pathology-related diagnostic errors, combining structured case-level data extraction with doctrinal legal analysis and clinical pathology interpretation.
